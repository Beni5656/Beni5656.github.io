CS180 · Programming Project #1

Images of the Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection: splitting each glass plate into its three exposures, aligning them, and stacking them back into colour.

OverviewWhat we are starting from

Around 1907 Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in colour, decades before colour printing was practical. His method was to record the same scene three times in quick succession through a blue, a green, and a red filter, onto a single tall glass plate. The Library of Congress digitised those plates, and each scan is one tall greyscale image holding three stacked frames, in the order B, G, R from top to bottom.

The job is to cut the plate into thirds and slide the green and red frames back on top of the blue one until they line up, which turns three greyscale exposures into a single colour photograph. Sliding is all we are allowed: a plain (x, y) translation, no rotation and no scaling.

That sounds easy and mostly is, but two things make it interesting. The offsets are large — on the full-resolution plates the red frame can sit 177 pixels away from the blue one — so a naive search is far too slow. And the three frames are not photographs of the same thing: they record different parts of the spectrum, so a bright red coat is bright in one frame and nearly black in another. Matching raw brightness between them is unreliable.

Part 1Scoring a candidate alignment

To pick an offset we need a number that says how well two frames line up. I score with the L2 norm, the Euclidean distance between the two overlapping patches: 

def euclidean_distance(im1, im2):
    return np.sqrt(np.sum(np.sum((im1 - im2) ** 2)))

Lower is better, and a perfect match would be zero. The other standard choice is normalized cross-correlation, which is the dot product of the two patches after each has been mean-subtracted and scaled to unit length. NCC is the better pick when the two images differ by a constant brightness or gain, because normalising removes exactly that difference. I did not need it, for the reason in the next paragraph.

Rather than feeding raw pixels to the L2 norm, I run each frame through a Sobel filter first and compare gradient magnitudes. This is the part that makes the brightness problem go away. A Sobel filter responds to edges — where the brightness changes — and it does not care how bright the region is on either side. The boundary of a roof is an edge in all three exposures even when the roof itself is light in one and dark in another. Aligning on edges rather than on brightness is why this works on the Emir of Bukhara, the image the project warns about, without any special handling.

Why the score is computed on a cropped window Every candidate offset is produced with np.roll, which wraps pixels around the edge instead of dropping them. The strip that wraps around is nonsense — it is content from the opposite side of the frame — so before scoring I cut a margin off all four sides and compare only the interior. This margin turns out to matter for a second and much more important reason, which is Part 4.

Part 2Single-scale alignment

The simplest working version is an exhaustive search. Take every offset in a window of [-15, 15] pixels in both directions, score each one, and keep the best. That is 31 × 31 = 961 candidates per channel, and it is what align() does: 

def align(channel1, channel2, num1, num2, margin):
    grad1 = sobel(channel1)
    grad2 = sobel(channel2)
    cropped2 = grad2[margin : -margin, margin : -margin]

    for i in dx:                                   # horizontal offsets
        for j in dy:                               # vertical offsets
            row_shift = np.roll(grad1, j, axis=0)
            col_shift = np.roll(row_shift, i, axis=1)
            cropped1  = col_shift[margin : -margin, margin : -margin]
            score = euclidean_distance(cropped1, cropped2)
            # keep the offset with the lowest score

Offsets are (x, y) = (horizontal, vertical), the amount the green or red frame is moved to land on blue. The pyramid finds exactly the same answer roughly five times faster, which is a useful sanity check that the two paths agree.

Part 3The image pyramid

Single-scale search collapses on the full-resolution plates, for two separate reasons. The first is correctness: melons.tif needs to move its red frame 177 pixels, and self_portrait.tif needs 176. A ±15 window simply cannot reach those, so no amount of waiting would find the right answer. The second is cost: widening the window to ±200 would mean 401 × 401 ≈ 161,000 candidate offsets, each one scored over an 11-megapixel frame.

The fix is to search coarse-to-fine. pyramid() halves the image over and over until it is under 200 pixels tall, runs the exhaustive ±15 search there, then walks back up. At each step up it doubles the offset it inherited — a shift of n pixels at half size is 2n at full size — and searches only a small ±5 correction around it: 

def pyramid(channel1, channel2):
    if channel2.shape[0] < 200:
        return align(channel1, channel2, 15, 15, margin=...)      # coarsest level
    else:
        dx, dy, score = pyramid(rescale(channel1, 0.5),
                                rescale(channel2, 0.5))           # recurse
        dx *= 2
        dy *= 2                                                   # scale back up
        # apply the inherited guess, then refine it by at most +-5
        col_shift = np.roll(np.roll(channel1, dy, axis=0), dx, axis=1)
        corrected_dx, corrected_dy, new_score = align(col_shift, channel2, 5, 5, margin=...)
        return dx + corrected_dx, dy + corrected_dy, new_score

A 3200-pixel-tall frame takes about six halvings to get under 200, so the whole search is one 961-candidate pass on a tiny image plus six 121-candidate passes. Because each level is a quarter the area of the one above it, the cost is dominated by the single pass over the full-resolution image, and the offsets it can reach grow by roughly a factor of two per level, which is how it reaches 177 pixels from a ±15 start.

Cost in practice emir.tif is a 9,627 × 3,702 plate, so each of its three frames is 3,209 × 3,702. Aligning both the green and the red frame to blue takes 44 seconds, comfortably inside the one-minute-per-image target, and all fourteen run in a single pass without any per-image tuning: the same parameters are used everywhere.

Part 4The bug that mattered: aligning borders instead of photographs

With the pyramid working, seven of the fourteen images still came out wrong. Some were subtly soft, and two — harvesters and self_portrait — were doubled so badly the whole frame looked embossed. The offsets were not random, though, and that was the clue. Measuring how far each output was still misaligned gave this:

Almost every error was horizontal, with the vertical component at or near zero. A search that was simply confused would not favour one axis so strongly, so something in the images had to be pulling sideways.

It was the plate borders. Each scanned frame is ringed by black and white edging, and those borders do not sit at the same place in all three exposures — they are mostly vertical strips, offset horizontally from frame to frame. A Sobel filter reports the strongest edge it can find, and the hard black-to-white transition of a border is a far stronger edge than anything in an actual photograph. So the search was quietly optimising the wrong thing: it was lining up the borders, and dragging the photograph out of alignment to do it.

The fix The align() function already cropped a margin off before scoring, to discard the np.roll wrap-around. Widening that margin to also exclude the outer 15% of the frame keeps the borders out of the score entirely, so the only edges left to match are the ones belonging to the photograph. That is the whole change — one extra variable, used in the two margin arguments. The search windows, the metric, and the recursion are all untouched. 
# ignore the outer 15% when scoring. the plate borders sit at a different
# offset in each of the three exposures, and sobel makes them the strongest
# edges in the frame, so otherwise we line up the borders, not the photo
border = int(min(channel2.shape) * 0.15)

All seven went to a residual of zero. The pairs below are the same crop, at full resolution, before and after.

Part 6What is still imperfect

Thirteen of the fourteen now measure a residual misalignment of exactly zero, and the last, religous_painting.tif, measures 3 pixels, which is well under a tenth of a percent of the frame width and is not visible. There are no remaining alignment failures to report. Three cosmetic things are worth being straight about, though.

The plate borders are still in the output. The fix in Part 4 keeps the borders out of the score, which is what alignment needed, but it does not remove them from the saved image, so the coloured edging is still visible around every frame. Detecting where each border ends and trimming to it is a separate piece of work.

The water in church.tif shows rainbow banding, and that one is not fixable by aligning. The three exposures were taken one after another, and the river surface moved in between, so the three frames genuinely record different water. No single translation can reconcile them. The test is whether the solid parts of the scene line up, and the stones, steps and building in that same frame do.

The contrast step is doing less than its name suggests. The function is called white_balance, but what it does is stretch each channel so that its 1st and 99th percentile land on 0 and 1. That is automatic contrast: it fixes exposure and restores punch, and it does help, but it is not white balance. Real white balance means estimating what colour the light was and cancelling it, which this does not attempt. 

def white_balance(im, lo=1, hi=99):
    out = np.zeros_like(im)
    for c in range(im.shape[2]):
        lo_v, hi_v = np.percentile(im[:, :, c], [lo, hi])
        out[:, :, c] = np.clip((im[:, :, c] - lo_v) / (hi_v - lo_v), 0, 1)
    return out

Percentiles rather than the true minimum and maximum, so that a few dust specks or a blown highlight cannot waste the entire range on outliers.
