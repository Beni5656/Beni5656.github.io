CS180 · Programming Project #2

Fun with Filters and Frequencies

Building 2D convolution from scratch, then using it for edge detection, image sharpening, hybrid images, and multiresolution blending.

Part 1.1Convolutions from scratch

I wrote 2D convolution two ways. The first uses four nested loops, two over the image and two over the kernel, accumulating one multiply at a time. The second keeps the two image loops but replaces the inner pair with a single vectorized multiply over the whole patch: 

patch = padded_image[img_h:img_h+kernel_height, img_w:img_w+kernel_width]
output[img_h, img_w] = np.sum(patch * flipped_kernel)

Both zero-pad by half the kernel size on each side so the output matches the input dimensions, and both flip the kernel before sliding it, which is what separates convolution from correlation. Checked against scipy.signal.convolve2d with mode='same' on a random 20×20 array: all three agree to floating-point tolerance.

Boundary handling I pad with zeros. That darkens the outermost rows and columns slightly, since those output pixels average real image data together with padded zeros. It's visible as a faint dark border on the blurred results below.

Applied to a photo of myself, downscaled 4× first because a per-pixel Python loop on the full 3088×2316 original is far too slow. Below: a 9×9 box blur, and the finite difference operators Dx = [1, 0, −1] and its transpose.

Part 1.2Finite difference operator

Convolving the cameraman with Dx and Dy gives the partial derivatives in each direction. Each one only responds to edges running perpendicular to it. Dx picks up the vertical tripod legs and building edges, while Dy picks up horizontal ones like the horizon.

Combining them into the gradient magnitude sqrt(Dx² + Dy²) gives an orientation-independent measure of edge strength, which I then threshold to get a binary edge map.

Picking the threshold 0.2 was chosen by eye as the point that keeps the coat, tripod and skyline while suppressing most of the grass. Lower values let a lot of grass noise through; higher values start breaking the tripod legs into dashes. There isn't a clean separation, because the grass texture genuinely has gradient magnitudes comparable to the weaker real edges, which is exactly the problem Part 1.3 addresses.

Part 1.3Derivative of Gaussian filter

Blurring first with a 9×9 Gaussian (σ = 1.5) and then differentiating gives a much cleaner result. The grass noise largely disappears, the remaining edges are thicker and smoother, and the binary edge map holds together as continuous contours instead of broken speckle.

The same result comes from a single convolution. Because convolution is associative, convolving the Gaussian with Dx first produces a derivative-of-Gaussian kernel that does both jobs in one pass:

Verifying the two are equivalent Comparing the one-step DoG result against the two-step blur-then-differentiate result with np.allclose returns True, but only away from the borders. Padding the raw image once makes different zero assumptions than padding the already-blurred image a second time, so I exclude a margin of half the combined kernel size on each side before comparing. The interiors match exactly.

Part 2.1Image sharpening

Unsharp masking works by isolating what a blur removes and adding it back amplified. Low-pass the image to get blurred, subtract to get the high-frequency detail, then add a scaled copy of that detail to the original: 

sharpened = f + alpha * (f - f*g) = (1 + alpha)*f - alpha*(f*g)

Since convolution is linear, this collapses into a single kernel: a scaled impulse minus a scaled Gaussian, which I build as (1 + alpha) * impulse − alpha * gaussian and apply in one pass per color channel.

Varying the sharpening amount

Higher alpha gives progressively more contrast at edges. By alpha = 3 the masonry detail is crisp, but halos start forming along high-contrast boundaries.

A second image

The whiskers, eye detail and nose edges all come up sharply. Worth noting the background grid pattern also gets amplified. The source is a photo of a screen, and unsharp masking boosts all high-frequency content, not just the detail you care about.

Sharpening a blurred image: the round trip

Observation Sharpening is not an inverse of blurring. The re-sharpened image is clearly crisper than the blurred one, since edges are boosted back, but it does not recover the original. Unsharp masking can only re-amplify high-frequency content that survived the blur; detail the Gaussian destroyed outright is gone and cannot be recreated. Fine texture stays missing, and the strong edges that do come back show slight ringing from the amplification.

Part 2.2Hybrid images

A hybrid image combines the high frequencies of one picture with the low frequencies of another. Up close your eye resolves fine detail and sees the first image; from a distance that detail is no longer resolvable and the blurred second image dominates. The high-pass is image − blur(image, sigma1), the low-pass is blur(image, sigma2), and the hybrid is their sum.

On alignment The provided align_images recenters each image by zero-padding until the eye midpoint is centered, then crops. For pairs with very different dimensions that padding is large, and it left black bars and a visible diagonal seam in my results. Instead I scale each image to a fixed target eye distance and crop a window around the eye midpoint that both images can fill, so every output pixel comes from real image data. For Derek and Nutmeg I fit a similarity transform to the two eye pairs, which also handles rotation. That is necessary here because Nutmeg's head is tilted about 27° relative to Derek's.

Derek + Nutmeg: sigma1 = 4, sigma2 = 12

Seal + panda: sigma1 = 4, sigma2 = 12

This pair needed a compromise on scale. The seal's eyes are 360px apart against a head 2.2× that width; the panda's are 128px apart against a head 3.5× that width. Matching eye distance exactly blew the panda up until its ears left the frame, so I scaled it by the geometric mean of the eye-matched and head-matched scales.

Cat + apple-person: sigma1 = 6, sigma2 = 14 (favorite result)

This is the strongest of the three, because the two images differ sharply in tone. The apple-person is dark with a red face on a black hood; the cat is bright cream. That contrast means the cat's whiskers read as bright strokes against a dark ground instead of washing out, which is what happened when I tried pairing two similarly pale subjects. Assignment matters too: the cat's identity lives in its whiskers and fur (high frequency) while the apple-person's lives in a red circle inside a black hood (low frequency), so putting each in its matching band is what makes the illusion work.

Part 2.3Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

A stack is a pyramid without the downsampling, so every level stays at the original resolution. Level 0 of the Gaussian stack is the input; each subsequent level is the previous one blurred again with the same kernel (49×49, σ = 8), so the blur compounds. The Laplacian stack is the difference between consecutive Gaussian levels, with the final level being the most-blurred Gaussian residual, so that summing the whole Laplacian stack reconstructs the original exactly.

The "oraple" column here is the naive splice: the left half of the apple concatenated to the right half of the orange with a hard cut. That is what motivates the blending in Part 2.4.

Why the middle Laplacian levels look faint Repeatedly blurring with the same kernel has diminishing returns. The effective blur grows like the square root of the number of passes, so the jump from level 0 to 1 adds far more smoothing than the jump from 3 to 4. The difference between two nearly converged levels is therefore small, which is why the later bands sit close to flat gray. It's also why the reference figure compares levels 0, 2 and 4 rather than consecutive ones.

Part 2.4Multiresolution blending

The trick is to blend each frequency band over a different spatial distance: fine detail switches over abruptly, broad color transitions over a wide region. That falls out naturally from building a Laplacian stack for each image, a Gaussian stack for the mask, and combining them level by level: 

blended_levels[i] = mask_stack[i] * lap_1[i] + (1 - mask_stack[i]) * lap_2[i]

Because the mask gets blurrier at each level alongside the images, the coarse bands get a wide transition while the finest band keeps a sharp one. Summing the blended stack collapses it back into the final image.

The oraple: vertical seam

Chess rooks: vertical seam

Both rooks are identical in shape, so this isolates the color transition completely. It's also an unforgiving test: near-black against near-white on a flat white background gives the seam nowhere to hide. Sampling a horizontal slice through the body confirms there is no step: brightness ramps smoothly from 0.27 to 0.87 across 80 pixels. Using a wider mask blur made it worse, not better, by pulling both halves toward mid-gray and destroying the contrast that makes the image interesting.

Soccer ball as the moon: irregular mask

The mask is found automatically rather than hardcoded: everything darker than 0.92 against the ball's white backdrop is the ball, which gives its bounding box and from that its center and radius. The circle is pulled 2px inside the rim so the white background doesn't get dragged into the dark sky. A small amount still bleeds through at the transition, but against a night sky that reads as atmospheric glow rather than as an artifact.

The process, level by level

Left column is the apple weighted by the mask, middle is the orange weighted by the inverted mask, right is their sum. Each band is stretched to its own range so the structure is visible. The higher bands have small amplitude and would otherwise render as flat gray. Zero maps to mid-gray under that normalization, which is why the masked-out half of each contribution appears neutral rather than black.

What I took away from this project The single most useful idea here is that an image is a sum of frequency bands you can manipulate independently. Sharpening, hybrid images and blending are all the same machinery pointed in different directions: pull apart the bands, change their weights, add them back. The second lesson was practical: most of my bad results came from bad alignment or badly matched source images, not from the filtering, and no amount of parameter tuning fixes a pair of images that don't belong together.
